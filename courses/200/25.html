Number

Number: what is it?

  • Does the verb change depending on how many are doing the action?
  • English: ‘The cat sleeps.’ / ‘The cats sleep.’
  • On a verb, number usually agrees with the subject

Number: Singular & Plural

Spanish English
como ‘I eat’
comes ‘you eat’
come ‘she eats’
comemos ‘we eat’
coméis ‘y’all eat’
comen ‘they eat’
  • Which endings mark plural?

Number: Unmarked

Japanese English
watashi wa taberu ‘I eat’
anata wa taberu ‘you eat’
kanojo wa taberu ‘she eats’
watashitachi wa taberu ‘we eat’
karera wa taberu ‘they eat’
  • taberu ‘eat’ never changes: Japanese verbs mark neither person nor number

Number: Singular, Plural, and Dual

Singular Dual Plural
1st bhavāmi
‘I am’		 bhavāvaḥ
‘we two are’		 bhavāmaḥ
‘we are’
2nd bhavasi
‘you are’		 bhavathaḥ
‘you two are’		 bhavatha
‘y’all are’
3rd bhavati
‘she is’		 bhavataḥ
‘they two are’		 bhavanti
‘they are’
  • Sanskrit bhū ‘be’: three persons × three numbers = nine endings

A rule of thumb

  • Your verbs should not have more numbers than your nouns
  • But you can have number on your nouns and not on your verbs

Naq’anir: we already decided this!

Naq’anir English
hu hût lí. ‘The man is good.’
hûtĩ lí. ‘Men are good.’
  • Plural subject, same verb: no number on the verb
  • Number is shown on the subject only: na lu ‘I go’, nã lu ‘we go’

Valency

Valency: what is it?

  • How many nominals (nouns or pronouns) does the verb need?
  • Count them:
    • ‘It’s raining.’
    • ‘She’s sleeping.’
    • ‘She’s hugging the wall.’
    • ‘She’s giving the people candy.’

Valency: the counts

Sentence Nominals Name
‘It’s raining.’ 0 (‘it’ is empty) avalent
‘She’s sleeping.’ 1: she intransitive
‘She’s hugging the wall.’ 2: she, the wall transitive
‘She’s giving the people candy.’ 3: she, the people, candy ditransitive
  • Last time: S V = intransitive, S V O = transitive

English doesn’t mark valency

  • The same verb can have different valencies:
Sentence Count
‘The ice melted.’ 1
‘The sun melted the ice.’ 2
  • Nothing on melted tells you which one you have

Some languages DO mark it

  • In Swahili, a suffix can add a nominal:
Swahili English Count
ninapika chakula. ‘I cook food.’ 2
ninampikia mtoto chakula. ‘I cook food for the child.’ 3
  • pika ‘cook’ → pik-i-a ‘cook for (someone)’
  • The -i- makes room for one more nominal: the child

Another way to add one: “make someone do it”

Japanese English
tabe-ru ‘eat’
tabe-sase-ru ‘make (someone) eat’
  • -sase- adds a new nominal: the person causing the eating
  • This is called a causative

Naq’anir: a causative from pa ‘give’

  • Add -pa ‘give’ to a verb: ‘give (someone) the action’
Verb + -pa Count
θi ‘die’ θipa ‘kill’ 1 → 2
kíʂ ‘see’ kíʂpa ‘show’ 2 → 3
  • hu hût θi. ‘The man dies.’
  • ʈʂa ʈʂáʈʂ θipa hu hût. ‘The robot kills the man.’

Voice

Voice: what is it?

  • Which nominal is the subject?
  • Compare:
    • ‘I baked the pizza.’
    • ‘The pizza was baked.’
    • ‘The pizza baked in the oven.’
  • Who did the baking? What’s the subject?

Voice: the three types

Sentence Subject Voice
‘I baked the pizza.’ the baker active
‘The pizza was baked.’ the thing baked passive
‘The pizza baked in the oven.’ the thing baked middle
  • Passive: the baker is left out (or tucked into ‘by me’)
  • Middle: the action happens to the subject, with no outside baker in mind

Voice changes valency!

Sentence Count
‘I baked the pizza.’ 2
‘The pizza was baked.’ 1
  • The passive takes away a nominal: the baker
  • So voice and valency go together:
    • Some affixes add a nominal (causative, ‘for someone’)
    • Some affixes take one away (passive)

Swahili: one root, three valencies

Swahili English Count
ninapika chakula. ‘I cook food.’ 2
ninampikia mtoto chakula. ‘I cook food for the child.’ 3
chakula kinapikwa. ‘The food is cooked.’ 1
  • pik-a ‘cook’ → pik-i-a ‘cook for’ (+1)
  • pik-a ‘cook’ → pik-w-a ‘be cooked’ (−1, passive)

Naq’anir: a passive from q’á- ‘get’

  • Remember q’ápâ ‘accept’ = ‘get-gift’?
  • Put q’á- ‘get’ before a verb: ‘get (verb)-ed’
    • Like English ‘I got seen’
Naq’anir English Count
na kíʂ hu hût. ‘I see the man.’ 2
hu hût q’ákíʂ. ‘The man is seen.’ 1

Naq’anir: adding and taking away

Affix Meaning Effect Example
-pa ‘give’ +1 θi ‘die’ → θipa ‘kill’
q’á- ‘get’ −1 kíʂ ‘see’ → q’ákíʂ ‘be seen’
  • No middle voice: ‘I see myself’ is just na kíʂ na ‘I see me’

Activity – decide: number, valency, voice

  • Number: Does your verb change for number? How?
  • Valency: Can an affix add a nominal? (‘make someone do’, ‘do for someone’)
  • Voice: Can an affix take away a nominal? (passive)
    • Do you have a middle voice?
  • For each affix: what does it look like, and where does it go?

Tense

Tense: what is it?

  • When does the action happen?
    • ‘I walked to school.’ (past)
    • ‘I walk to school.’ (present)
    • ‘I will walk to school.’ (future)

Tense: how many?

  • Languages split up time in different ways:
Tenses The split Example
0 no tense at all Mandarin
2 past vs. non-past Finnish
2 future vs. non-future Dyirbal (Australia)
3 past, present, future Spanish
4 past, remote past, present, future Mɤɧøɴ (a conlang)
  • Let’s look at each one

Three tenses: Spanish

Spanish English
comí ‘I ate’
como ‘I eat’
comeré ‘I will eat’
  • Past, present and future each get their own ending
  • This is the system most English speakers expect

Two tenses: past vs. non-past (Finnish)

Finnish Word for word English
Luin eilen. I.read.PAST yesterday ‘I read yesterday.’
Luen nyt. I.read.NONPAST now ‘I’m reading now.’
Luen huomenna. I.read.NONPAST tomorrow ‘I’ll read tomorrow.’
  • Finnish has no future ending: the same form covers ‘now’ and ‘tomorrow’
  • A time word like huomenna ‘tomorrow’ tells you it’s the future

Two tenses: future vs. non-future

  • The opposite split: the future gets its own marker, and the past and present share one form
When Tense Marked?
yesterday non-future no marker
right now non-future no marker
tomorrow future marker
  • Dyirbal, a language of Australia, works like this
  • So will Naq’anir (coming up!)

Four tenses: a remote past (Mɤɧøɴ)

Tense Marker Mɤɧøɴ English
Present (nothing) kɤʛu meɴ. ‘I drink water.’
Future -ti kɤʛuti meɴ. ‘I will drink water.’
Past -pa kɤʛupa meɴ. ‘I drank water.’
Remote past -paa kɤʛupaa meɴ. ‘I drank water a long time ago.’
  • Two pasts: ‘recently’ (-pa) vs. ‘a long time ago’ (-paa)

No tense at all: Mandarin

Mandarin Word for word English
wǒ měitiān qù. I every.day go ‘I go every day.’
wǒ míngtiān qù. I tomorrow go ‘I’ll go tomorrow.’
  • The verb qù ‘go’ never changes
  • Time words do the work

Explore more tenses

  • https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammatical_tense
  • Scroll down to “Possible tenses”
  • Which split would fit your culture?
    • Does it matter to them whether something happened today or long ago?
    • Is the future special?

Naq’anir: future vs. non-future

  • Two tenses:
    • Non-future (past and present together): no marker
      • na sasa = ‘I talk’ or ‘I talked’
    • Future: put lu ‘go’ before the verb
      • Like English ‘I’m going to talk’
Naq’anir English
na sasa. ‘I talk.’ / ‘I talked.’
na lu sasa. ‘I will talk.’

Aspect

Aspect: what is it?

  • How does the action unfold over time?
    • Is it finished? Is it ongoing? Does it repeat?
  • Tense = when; aspect = how

Aspect in English

Sentence Name Meaning
‘I walked to school.’ simple past it happened
‘I have walked to school.’ perfect it’s done (and that matters now)
‘I was walking to school.’ progressive it was ongoing
  • All three talk about the past, but each one views the walking differently: that’s aspect

Aspect in Spanish

Spanish English Aspect
comí ‘I ate’ (once, done) completed
comía ‘I was eating’ / ‘I used to eat’ ongoing or habitual
  • Both are past tense; the ending tells you how the eating went

Aspect without tense: Mandarin

Mandarin Word for word English
wǒ chī le. I eat DONE ‘I ate.’ / ‘I’ve eaten.’
wǒ zài chī. I ONGOING eat ‘I’m eating.’
  • Mandarin has no tense, but it does have aspect

Naq’anir: completed with qup

  • Put qup before the verb: the action is done
    • qup is a brand-new syllable, not built from another word
    • In building affixes, you can choose an existing word from your lexicon if it makes sense, or just randomly generate one
Naq’anir English
na sasa. ‘I talk.’ / ‘I talked.’
na qup sasa. ‘I have (already) talked.’
  • Repeated action is already in the lexicon: sa ‘say’ → sasa ‘talk’

Mood

Mood: what is it?

  • What is the speaker doing with the sentence?
    • ‘I am a fish.’
    • ‘If I were a fish, I would eat fish food.’
    • ‘Be a fish!’
    • ‘Am I a fish?’

Mood: four common ones

Sentence Purpose Mood
‘I am a fish.’ statement indicative
‘If I were a fish, I would eat fish food.’ hypothetical subjunctive
‘Be a fish!’ command imperative
‘Am I a fish?’ question interrogative
  • English uses were, word order, and dropping ‘you’

Mood in Latin

Latin English Mood
Piscis sum. ‘I am a fish.’ statement
Sī piscis essem, cibum ederem. ‘If I were a fish, I would eat food.’ hypothetical
Es piscis! ‘Be a fish!’ command
Piscisne sum? ‘Am I a fish?’ question
  • Each mood changes the verb (sum, essem, es), or adds -ne

Naq’anir: mood

Mood How Naq’anir English
Statement (nothing) ʂi sasa. ‘You talk.’
Command drop the subject sasa! ‘Talk!’
Question hâ at the start hâ ʂi sasa? ‘Do you talk?’
Hypothetical sǎ ‘if’ at the start sǎ ʂi sasa… ‘If you talk…’
  • hâ comes from the question words: hâ ‘what’, hâr ‘where’, hâs ‘why’
  • Remember: Naq’anir always needs a subject, so a missing subject stands out

Activity – decide: tense, aspect, mood

  • Tense: How many tenses? How are they marked?
  • Aspect: Do you mark finished vs. ongoing? Repeated?
  • Mood: How do you make a command? A question? A hypothetical?
  • For each one: an affix, a separate word, word order, or nothing?

Putting It Together

Naq’anir verbs: the whole system

Property Naq’anir
Person not marked; honorific -tâ
Number not marked
Valency -pa ‘give’ adds one (causative)
Voice q’á- ‘get’ takes one away (passive)
Tense non-future: nothing; future: lu ‘go’ before the verb
Aspect completed: qup before the verb
Mood command: no subject; question: hâ; ‘if’: sǎ

Notice where the Naq’anir markers came from

  • Most markers were words first:
    • pa ‘give’ → causative -pa
    • q’á ‘get’ → passive q’á-
    • lu ‘go’ → future
    • tâl ‘Lord’ → honorific -tâ
  • Real languages do this all the time: English going to → gonna
  • But a marker can also just be invented: completed qup

Before Friday: 1. Person

  • Decide: Does your verb change depending on who does the action (‘I’, ‘you’, ‘she’)?
    • If yes: is it a prefix, a suffix, or something else?
    • Can the subject be dropped?
    • Optional: does the verb mark respect (like Korean -si-)?
  • Write down: your person markers, or “none”
  • Examples: ‘I eat’, ‘you eat’, ‘she eats’
  • Model: Naq’anir: none (na lu, ʂi lu); honorific ‑tâ

Before Friday: 2. Number

  • Decide: Does your verb change depending on how many do the action?
    • Use no more numbers than your nouns have
    • Is number fused with person (Spanish -mos), or separate?
  • Write down: your number markers, or “none”
  • Examples: ‘she eats’ vs. ‘they eat’ (and ‘they two eat’, if you have a dual)
  • Model: Naq’anir has none (hûtĩ lí ‘men are good’)

Before Friday: 3. Valency

  • Decide: Can a marker add a nominal to a verb?
    • ‘make someone do it’ (causative)
    • ‘do it for someone’ (like Swahili -i-)
  • Write down: the marker and where it goes, or “none”
  • Examples: one verb before and after: ‘die’ → ‘kill’; ‘eat’ → ‘feed’
  • Model: Naq’anir -pa ‘give’: θi ‘die’ → θipa ‘kill’

Before Friday: 4. Voice

  • Decide: Can a marker take away a nominal?
    • Passive: ‘The fish was eaten.’
    • Middle or reflexive: ‘The snake ate itself.’
  • Write down: the marker and where it goes, or “active only”
  • Examples: ‘I eat the fish’ → ‘The fish is eaten’
  • Model: Naq’anir q’á- ‘get’: kíʂ ‘see’ → q’ákíʂ ‘be seen’

Before Friday: 5. Tense

  • Decide: How does your language split up time?
    • None? Past vs. non-past? Future vs. non-future? Past, present, future? A remote past?
  • Write down: each tense and its marker
  • Examples: ‘I ate’, ‘I eat’, ‘I will eat’ (plus ‘I ate long ago’, if you have a remote past)
  • Model: Naq’anir non-future (no marker) vs. future lu: na lu sasa ‘I will talk’

Before Friday: 6. Aspect

  • Decide: Do you mark how the action unfolds?
    • Finished? Ongoing? Repeated? Habitual?
  • Write down: each aspect and its marker, or “none”
  • Examples: ‘I ate’ vs. ‘I was eating’; ‘I have eaten’
  • Model: Naq’anir completed qup: na qup sasa ‘I have talked’

Before Friday: 7. Mood

  • Decide: How do you mark:
    • a command? (‘Eat!’)
    • a question? (‘Do you eat?’)
    • a hypothetical? (‘If I ate…’)
  • Write down: the marker for each (affix, word, word order, or dropping the subject)
  • Examples: one sentence for each mood
  • Model: Naq’anir command = no subject (sasa!), question = hâ, ‘if’ = sǎ
