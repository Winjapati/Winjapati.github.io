Overview of Verbs

What is a verb?

  • A word for an action, an event, or a state
    • Action: lu ‘go’, sa ‘say’, pa ‘give’
    • Event: θi ‘die’
    • State: lí ‘be good’, ʐíq ‘be new’

Remember Naq’anir adjectives?

  • Last week we decided Naq’anir has no separate adjectives
  • ‘good’, ‘new’, ‘defective’ are all state verbs:
    • hu hût lí. ‘The man is good.’ (lit. ‘the man goods’)
  • So everything we decide about verbs today applies to them, too

Information Encoded in Verbs

  • Person
  • Number
  • Valency
  • Mood
  • Voice
  • Aspect
  • Tense

Key Takeaway

NONE of these things have to be encoded in the verb through affixation, but they do have to be marked somehow.

Today vs. Wednesday

  • Today: Person and Number
  • Wednesday: Valency, Mood, Voice, Aspect, Tense

Two sentence shapes for today

  • Some verbs need only a subject (S V):
    • ‘I sleep.’ ‘The robot walks.’
  • Some verbs need a subject and an object (S V O):
    • ‘I see you.’ ‘The robot helps us.’
  • (More on this on Wednesday, when we get to valency)

Person

Person

  • Consider the following sentences:
    • I sleep.
    • You sleep.
    • He/she/it sleeps.
    • We sleep.
    • Y’all sleep.
    • They sleep.
  • What changes in the verb?

Person in English

  • Almost nothing!
    • Only the 3rd person singular is different: sleep-s
  • The work of showing person is done by the pronoun: I, you, she…

Person in Spanish

  • Every person has its own ending

So Spanish can drop the subject

Spanish English
Como. ‘I eat.’
Comes. ‘You eat.’
Comemos. ‘We eat.’
  • The verb already tells you who is eating
  • In English, *Eat. can’t mean ‘I eat.’
  • Some noun phrase or pronoun is ENCODED within the verb

Beyond subjects: Georgian

  • Georgian verbs mark the object as well as the subject

Korean: no person, but respect

Korean English
jeo-neun gamnida. ‘I go.’
chingu-ga gamnida. ‘My friend goes.’
seonsaengnim-kkeseo gasimnida. ‘The teacher goes.’
  • What changes in the verb? Why?
  • Korean verbs don’t mark person or number, but they can mark that the subject is someone you respect: -si-
  • Compare Naq’anir: speakers use gender to make a point

A conlang that marks person: Mɤɧøɴ

  • Person markers are prefixed to the verb: subject + object + verb
sg. du. pl.
1 kɤ kuu kaa
2 ku kuu kwa
3 ‘metra’ o ow oa
3 ‘animal’ ʀɤ ʀuu ʀaa
3 ‘plant’ sø suu saa
3 ‘it’ ta taw taa

Mɤɧøɴ: subject and object on the verb

Mɤɧøɴ English
kɤʛu ‘I drink.’
kuʛu ‘You drink.’
oʛu ‘It (metra) drinks.’
kɤtaʛu ‘I drink it.’
kutaʛu ‘You drink it.’
otaʛu ‘It drinks it.’

Mɤɧøɴ: no extra pronoun needed

  • If the object is a full noun, the object marker drops out:
    • kɤʛu meɴ. ‘I drink water.’
    • not *kɤtaʛu meɴ.

Naq’anir: how could we mark person?

  • Option A: the verb never changes; a pronoun does the work (like English)
    • na lu ‘I go’, ʂi lu ‘you go’, hu lu ‘he/she goes’
  • Option B: the pronoun becomes a prefix (like Mɤɧøɴ)
    • nalu ‘I go’, ʂilu ‘you go’, hulu ‘he/she goes’
    • then the subject could be dropped, like Spanish

Naq’anir: we already decided!

  • Look back at Day 23:
    • hu hût lí. ‘The man is good.’
  • No prefix on lí: the verb doesn’t change for person
  • Option A it is:
    • The verb never changes for person
    • So the subject can never be dropped: *lu can’t mean ‘I go’

Naq’anir: but what about respect?

  • Like Korean, Naq’anir can mark that the subject is someone to respect
  • Where could an honorific come from?
    • tâl ‘Lord’ already gave us tâ, the divine ‘the’
    • It can give us a verb suffix, too: -tâ

Naq’anir: the honorific -tâ

Naq’anir English
hu hût sasa. ‘The man speaks.’
p’ir sasatâ. ‘The Board speaks.’ (respectfully)
tâl kíʂtâ nã. ‘The Lord sees us.’
málʈʂiq sasatâ. ‘His Highness Malchekk speaks.’ (mocking)
  • Just like the divine gender, speakers can use -tâ to make a point

  • If so, how? (Prefix? Suffix? Something else?)
  • Can the subject be dropped?
  • Does your verb mark respect (like Korean -si- or Naq’anir -tâ)?

Number

Number

  • Remember number from nominals?
  • On a verb, number usually agrees with the subject:
    • ‘The cat sleeps.’ / ‘The cats sleep.’

Number: Singular & Plural

Spanish English
como ‘I eat’
comes ‘you eat’
come ‘she eats’
comemos ‘we eat’
coméis ‘y’all eat’
comen ‘they eat’
  • Which endings mark plural?

Number: Unmarked

  • Japanese verbs mark neither person nor number

Number: Singular, Plural, and Dual

  • Sanskrit: three persons × three numbers, in every tense and mood

A rule of thumb

  • Your verbs should not have more numbers than your nominal system
  • But you can have overt number in your nouns and not in your verbs

Mɤɧøɴ: where did the markers come from?

  • Mɤɧøɴ already had 3 numbers in the noun system: singular, dual, plural
  • Here are the pronouns:
sg. du. pl.
1 kɤ kɤɠu kɤka
2 ku kuɠu kuka
3 ‘metra’ o oɠu oka
3 ‘animal’ ʀɤ ʀɤɠu ʀɤka
3 ‘plant’ sø søɠu søka
3 ‘it’ ta taɠu taka

Mɤɧøɴ: compare pronouns and verb markers

du. pronoun du. marker pl. pronoun pl. marker
1 kɤɠu kuu kɤka kaa
2 kuɠu kuu kuka kwa
3 ‘metra’ oɠu ow oka oa
3 ‘animal’ ʀɤɠu ʀuu ʀɤka ʀaa
3 ‘plant’ søɠu suu søka saa
3 ‘it’ taɠu taw taka taa
  • What changed?

Mɤɧøɴ: what changed?

  • The consonants ɠ and k dropped out
  • The vowels that were left merged:
    • kɤɠu → kɤu → kuu
    • kɤka → kɤa → kaa
  • The pronouns wore down into prefixes: this is how real agreement markers are born

Naq’anir: how could we mark number?

  • Naq’anir nouns have two numbers, marked by nasalization or -ĩ / -tĩ
  • We could nasalize the verb when the subject is plural:
    • hûtĩ lũ ‘men go’ (hypothetical)
  • But look back at Day 23 again…

Naq’anir: we already decided this, too!

Naq’anir English
hu hût lí. ‘The man is good.’
hûtĩ lí. ‘Men are good.’
  • Plural subject, same verb: no number agreement
  • Number is shown on the subject only: na lu ‘I go’, nã lu ‘we go’

Final decision: Naq’anir

  • Person: not marked on the verb
  • Number: not marked on the verb
  • The verb never changes for person or number
  • A subject is always required
  • Respect: the suffix -tâ when the subject is honored (or mocked)

Activity – number: decide

  • How many numbers does your nominal system have?
  • Does your verb mark number? All of them, or fewer?
  • If so, how? Is it tied to the person marker (like Spanish -mos, Mɤɧøɴ kaa), or separate?

Beginning to Translate

Naq’anir: S V

  • What do these mean?
Naq’anir English
na lu. ?
ʂi sasa. ?
hu hût lulu. ?
nã huʂ. ?
ʈʂã sasa. ?
  • Vocabulary:
    • Pronouns: na ‘I’, ʂi ‘you’, ʈʂa ‘it (machine)’
    • Plural: nasalize the vowel (na ‘I’ → nã ‘we’)
    • hu ‘the (human)’, hût ‘man’
    • Verbs: lu ‘go’, sasa ‘talk’, lulu ‘walk’, huʂ ‘leave’

Naq’anir: S V

Naq’anir Gloss English
na lu. I go ‘I go.’
ʂi sasa. you talk ‘You talk.’
hu hût lulu. the.HUM man walk ‘The man walks.’
nã huʂ. I.PL leave ‘We leave.’
ʈʂã sasa. it.MACH.PL talk ‘They (machines) talk.’
  • HUM = human gender, MACH = machine gender, PL = plural

Naq’anir: S V O

  • For today, the order is Subject–Verb–Object, like English
    • This is provisional: we’ll decide word order for real next week
  • No case, so a pronoun looks the same as subject or object:
    • na = ‘I’ and ‘me’
