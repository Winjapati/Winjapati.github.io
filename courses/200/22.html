How is gender marked?

  • On the noun itself, through affixation (Swahili m-tu ‘person’, ki-tu ‘thing’)
  • Through agreement on other words:
    • Modifiers: adjectives and articles (Spanish el gato negro / la casa negra)
    • Verbs (Russian on spal ‘he slept’ / ona spala ‘she slept’)
    • Pronouns only (English he / she / it)
  • Through classifiers (separate grammatical words)

In Naq’anir: three genders (or four)

Gender ‘the’ Comes from Examples
Human hu hût ‘man’ hût ‘man’, hûtĩ ‘people’, niríθa ‘Nairetha’
Machine ʈʂa ʈʂáʈʂ ‘robot’ ʈʂáʈʂ ‘robot’, ʈʂaq’ʈʂaq’ ‘motor’, ʈʂál ‘pillar’
Neuter ju q’aʂ ‘building’, kiʂ ‘eye’, lap’ ‘table’
Divine tâ tâl ‘Lord’ tâl ‘Lord’, tálu ‘universe’, tâlq’aʂ ‘church’
  • The divine gender is mostly only used by religious speakers, though can be used in a mocking fashion by non-believers

In Naq’anir: how is it marked?

  • With a different word for ‘the’ for each gender, placed before the noun
    • Like a compound, the modifier comes first: tâl-q’aʂ ‘Lord-building’ = ‘church’
‘a …’ ‘the …’
hût hu hût
ʈʂáʈʂ ʈʂa ʈʂáʈʂ
q’aʂ ju q’aʂ
tâl tâ tâl
  • There is no word for ‘a’: an indefinite noun is just the bare noun
  • Most of the words for ‘the’ come from nouns: hu from hût, ʈʂa from ʈʂáʈʂ, tâ from tâl

Speakers use gender to make a point

  • ‘Malchekk speaks to Sidiki.’
    • málʈʂiq: just ‘Malchekk’, no comment
    • hu málʈʂiq: ‘the (human) Malchekk’: he is still a person
    • ʈʂa málʈʂiq: ‘the (machine) Malchekk’: he has become a machine
    • ju málʈʂiq: ‘the (thing) Malchekk’: he is not even a person, just a thing
    • tâ málʈʂiq: ‘the (divine) Malchekk’: mocking his claim that the Lord gives him his powers

Speakers use gender to make a point

  • ‘the universe’: ju tálu (neuter) from a non-believer, tâ tálu (divine) from a believer

This is Case!

Case

  • A noun’s role or function in the sentence: how the noun is related to the verb

Case

  • The most common cases are SUBJECT, DIRECT OBJECT and INDIRECT OBJECT
    • Subject: the nominal that performs the verb
    • Direct object (DO): the nominal that is affected by the verb
    • Indirect object (IO): the nominal that is the target, recipient or direction of the verb

Case in English

  • How does it work in English?
    • Nominative vs. accusative (subject vs. object): I vs. me, he vs. him, etc.
    • Genitive (possessive): my, mine, your, yours, etc.

Latin Case

  • The ending on the noun indicates its role in the sentence:
    • Nominative endings mark the subject, the thing verbing
    • Accusative endings mark the direct object, the thing being verbed
Latin English
Agricola poetam videt. ‘The farmer sees the poet.’
Poeta agricolam videt. ‘The poet sees the farmer.’

Noun Endings

  • Some languages, like Finnish, have easily identifiable affixes (word parts that attach) for each case.

Finnish Case System: Location

Finnish English
Kissa on talossa. ‘The cat is in the house.’
Kissa on talolla. ‘The cat is at the house.’
Kissa tulee talosta. ‘The cat comes out of the house.’
Kissa tulee talolta. ‘The cat comes from the house.’
Kissa menee taloon. ‘The cat goes into the house.’
Kissa menee talolle. ‘The cat goes to the house.’

Case or no case?

  • Are subjects, objects, and/or indirect objects marked differently?
    • these are called grammatical cases
  • Do affixes mark location, source, instrument, possession, or do separate words (aka prepositions)?
    • these are called prepositional cases

In Naq’anir: no grammatical cases

  • The lexicon already points this way: pronouns have one form each
    • na = ‘I’, ‘me’ and ‘my’
    • su = ‘he’, ‘him’, and ‘his’
  • Subject & object: marked by word order, not case
    • What’s the word order?
    • We’ll build that in a couple weeks.

In Naq’anir: no prepositional cases, only prepositions

  • Location, source, instrument, possession: marked with prepositions from the lexicon
Meaning Preposition Example Gloss
Location ul ‘in’ ul q’aʂ ‘in the building’
Location ra ‘at’ ra lap’ ‘at the table’
Source lǎ ‘from’ lǎ tâlq’aʂ ‘from the church’
Instrument wa ‘with’ wa kiʂ ‘with an eye’
Possession ʐa ‘of’ kiʂ ʐa málʈʂiq ‘Malchekk’s eye’
  • lǎ ‘from’ is new: the script never needed it, so it was made from random syllables, just like a LanguaGen word
  • Word order isn’t final yet: kiʂ ʐa málʈʂiq ‘eye of Malchekk’ puts the possessor after the noun, but elsewhere the modifier comes first (tâl-q’aʂ). We’ll settle this when we build word order.
